# Storylines: how a wall reference is made

Nov 11, 2026. An explainer of `tools/refs/wallrefs.py`, told through one real wall: the 8.9 m street wall of `plateau:bldg_5b434eb5-e19c-4ccb-858a-3b2d1f6024f2` in Oshiage 2. None is built until one is picked.

The one idea: a few strangers' street photos can be turned into one true-scale, front-on picture of a real wall.

Facts available (from the tool's own run): 2,364 Mapillary photos around the block; 40 used for this building, 58 of 60 placed in the first run; Mapillary's positions disagree between photo sequences by metres; after fitting to the building outlines the wall's plane sits 0.04 m from PLATEAU's outline; six photos combined; 80 pixels per metre; 78% of the wall seen.

Assumptions for all three: 45 to 60 s, 1920 by 1080, no voiceover, music and sound effects from free sources, an on-screen credit "Photos: Mapillary contributors, CC BY-SA 4.0".

## A. Nobody took this photo (recommended)

- Device and form: the result first, then one worked example; one continuous take that pulls back from the wall to the map and dives in again.
- What the viewer sees: the finished front-on wall, then the question of where it came from, answered step by step on the real street. It feels like watching a magic trick explained.
- First two seconds: the finished wall image fills the frame, sharp and square-on. The line "Nobody took this photo" sets over it.
- Beats: finished wall → pull back to six skewed street photos it came from → up to the map, photo positions as dots along the street → the dots are wrong: sequences jump metres apart → the reconstruction slides onto the building outlines and locks → the wall's plane lights, "4 cm from the map" → photos flatten onto it one by one → combined: wires fade out → "A reference to match. Not a texture to copy."
- Look: daylight. The block as the project's grey blockout on a pale ground, real photos as the only colour, one orange accent for the wall being worked on, a plain grotesque type.
- Sound: one quiet pulse of music; a shutter click per photo, a soft lock when the outlines snap, a low swell on the combined image.

## B. The projector room

- Device and form: the object is the explanation; a flight through one 3D world.
- What the viewer sees: a tabletop model of the street where every camera position is a small projector, each throwing its photo onto the blank model wall. The picture on the wall sharpens as projectors are moved to their true places. It feels like a workshop demonstration.
- First two seconds: a blank grey model wall in a model street; one projector clicks on and a skewed photo lands across it, wrong.
- Beats: one projector, wrong → forty projectors along the street, a mess of overlapping light → projectors shuffle as the model corrects their positions → the wall plane slides 4 cm → images register, the wall comes into focus → the truck in three beams cancels out → the camera turns to face the wall square-on: the reference.
- Look: a paper-and-card street model under a soft top light, projector beams as visible cones, photos only where light lands.
- Sound: room tone, projector fans, a click per lamp, music entering when the image registers.

## C. Six bad photos

- Device and form: before and after; split screen that collapses to one.
- What the viewer sees: six street photos of the same building in six panes, each with something in the way. Each pane is corrected in turn and they fold into a single clean wall. It feels brisk, like a recipe.
- First two seconds: six panes snap in on the beat, each a skewed photo with its fault circled: truck, wires, tree, angle, glare, crop.
- Beats: six faults → pane one un-skews to a rectangle with a metre ruler along its base → the other five follow, faster → the panes stack and the median wipes the wires → a size check against the map, "8.9 m wide, 80 px per metre" → one clean wall → the line.
- Look: flat colour fields behind each pane, bold condensed type, hard cuts.
- Sound: a percussive track, cuts on the beat, one hit per pane.
